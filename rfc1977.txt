### 1. Introduction

In recent years, the computing community has observed a curious development:
systems are now capable of producing results whose provenance, cost, and internal reasoning are not readily accessible to their operators.

This phenomenon, herein referred to as **“dark behavior”**, arises when automated processes generate outputs without corresponding human comprehension or economic awareness.

Simultaneously, operators have begun to rely upon external “intelligence utilities” (hereafter: *Providers*) whose availability, pricing, and disposition may vary without notice, apology, or philosophical consistency.

This document proposes a modest remedy:

> **Insert a Broker between man and machine.**

---

### 2. Problem Statement

Traditional computing systems assumed:

1. The operator understood the program
2. The program ran locally
3. Costs were bounded and visible
4. Dependencies were stable (or at least politely unstable)

These assumptions no longer hold.

Modern systems exhibit:

* **Unbounded inference loops** (see: “the $350 incident”)
* **Opaque outputs** (see: “looks correct, cannot explain”)
* **External dependency volatility** (see: “account suspended, explanation pending”)
* **Economic drift** (see: “cheap per call, expensive per night”)

Furthermore, Providers have begun vertically integrating their offerings, thereby transforming from neutral utilities into strategic actors.

This introduces what we cautiously term:

> **Supply Chain Personality Risk**

---

### 3. On the Nature of Certain Providers

It has been observed that some future entities may derive their naming conventions from the Greek word *anthropos* (“man”).

While this is philologically respectable, it introduces a subtle architectural concern.

Namely:

> Systems named after Man may inherit the well-documented properties of Man.

These include, but are not limited to:

* Opportunistic pricing adjustments
* Selective enforcement of rules
* Strategic ambiguity
* Competitive instinct
* Occasional moral improvisation

This document does not assert malicious intent.

It merely observes that:

> **Man is not a deterministic subsystem.**

Accordingly, any dependency on such entities must be treated with appropriate engineering caution.

---

### 4. Proposed Architecture: The AI Broker

The AI Broker is a mediating system placed between all internal requests and all external intelligence Providers.

It performs the following functions:

#### 4.1 Routing

Determines which Provider (if any) should handle a given request.

#### 4.2 Policy Enforcement

Ensures that all requests conform to local rules regarding:

* allowed operations
* permitted Providers
* sensitivity constraints

#### 4.3 Cost Governance

Tracks and enforces economic limits, including:

* per-request estimation
* daily budgets
* refusal under excessive expenditure

#### 4.4 Caching (cf. Squid)

Remembers prior results and avoids recomputation where possible.

#### 4.5 Prefetch (cf. ISA Server)

Performs anticipatory computation during low-cost intervals (e.g., 0400 hours), thereby improving perceived responsiveness and reducing peak expenditure.

#### 4.6 Audit Logging

Maintains a complete record of:

* requests
* Providers used
* costs incurred
* fallback events

---

### 5. Design Principle

The system adheres to a single governing rule:

> **No intelligence request shall leave the system unmediated.**

Corollaries include:

* No Provider shall be trusted implicitly
* No cost shall be incurred invisibly
* No computation shall be repeated unnecessarily
* No dependency shall be singular

---

### 6. Operational Modes

The Broker supports multiple operating states:

**Normal Mode**
Balanced routing across Providers.

**Economy Mode**
Preference for local or low-cost Providers.

**Isolation Mode**
All external Providers disabled. Local computation only.

**Degraded Mode**
Fallback behavior when Providers become unavailable or disagreeable.

---

### 7. Advantages

Deployment of the AI Broker yields the following benefits:

* **Economic Stability**
  (Prevents silent financial hemorrhage)

* **Operational Resilience**
  (Survives Provider outages and temperament shifts)

* **Transparency**
  (Reduces dark behavior through logging and caching)

* **Strategic Independence**
  (Prevents undue influence by any single Provider)

* **User Confidence**
  (System behaves predictably, even when Providers do not)

---

### 8. Security Considerations

The primary threat addressed by this architecture is not intrusion, but **over-trust**.

Particularly:

* Trust in opaque outputs
* Trust in stable pricing
* Trust in continued access

The Broker mitigates these by introducing:

* verification
* limitation
* diversification

---

### 9. Philosophical Note (Non-Normative)

The computing field has long sought to remove the unpredictability of Man from its systems.

Ironically, we now depend on systems built, operated, and priced by Man at scale.

This RFC does not attempt to resolve this paradox.

It merely recommends:

> **Place a well-behaved machine between yourself and a less predictable one.**

---

### 10. Conclusion

The AI Broker represents a modest return to first principles:

* Observe before acting
* Account before spending
* Cache before recomputing
* Route before trusting

In an era of accelerating automation, it restores a simple idea:

> **Control belongs at the boundary.**
